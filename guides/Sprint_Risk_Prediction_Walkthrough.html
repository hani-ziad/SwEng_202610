1 · Start here

If you have never seen this project, read this first

In short

We taught a computer to look at a software team's plan on the first day of a sprint and say how likely the sprint is to run over. The hard part was making sure the computer only saw what the team could have seen that morning.

How to read this page

Every section opens with an "In short" box. Read only those and you have the whole story in five minutes. The full page takes about half an hour. Figures with a Play or Show button are animated: press them. Any word you don't know is in the glossary at the end.

Scrum in one minute

A Scrum team keeps a backlog, a list of work items called issues. Every one to four weeks it starts a sprint: at a planning meeting it picks the issues it commits to finish and, often, gives each a size estimate in story points. Then it works. At the planned end the team closes the sprint and anything unfinished rolls over.

Jira in one minute

Jira is the tool most teams use to run this. Each issue has fields (status, sprint, story points, assignee). Jira also keeps a change log: every time a field changes, it records from what, to what, and when. That change log is the raw material of this whole project.

What "predicting" means here

A model is a formula learned from past sprints. You give it a handful of numbers about a new sprint (its features) and it returns a risk score. To learn the formula the model is shown old sprints together with how they ended (their labels). To check it honestly, it is then tested on sprints it never saw during learning.

One model, one outcome. The project predicts two different things, spillover and late closure (section 2 explains both). A model only ever predicts one of them. So every type of model is trained twice, once for each outcome, and every result on this page says which outcome it is about. In the results charts, blue is late closure and orange is spillover.

The one rule that runs through everything: no hindsight. Nothing recorded after the moment of prediction may reach the model. Most of this project is about the ways that rule is easy to break by accident, and what breaking it costs.
2 · The question

Two ways a sprint misses its boundary

In short

A sprint can fail in two recorded ways: work left unfinished (spillover) or the sprint closed late in Jira (late closure). We ask whether either can be predicted on day one, and whether watching the sprint helps.

When a sprint goes wrong, Jira records it in two ways. Both can be read straight from the data, with no one having to judge the team.

Spillover

At least one issue that was committed at the start is still unresolved at the planned end. Work rolled into the next sprint.

Late closure

Someone presses "Complete sprint" in Jira more than one day after the planned end date. This is about when the sprint was formally closed, which is not the same as when the work was done.

The research question has two parts: on the first day of a sprint, can we tell which sprints are most likely to spill over or close late? And once the sprint is running, does watching its progress help? What makes this hard is the no-hindsight rule from section 1: the prediction may only use information that existed at that moment.

Both outcomes are common. 38.7% of sprints closed late, and 93.1% of sprints that had work in them spilled over. Because almost every sprint spills a little, the useful question for spillover is not "will anything be left over?" but "which sprints are most at risk?"
3 · The data

TAWOS, and how 2,657 sprint rows became 2,602

In short

Eight open-source projects from the public TAWOS dataset. Of 2,657 sprints, 55 were removed (still running, broken dates, or no history), leaving 2,602 for late closure. For spillover, the 911 sprints with no work in them are also left out, leaving 1,691.

TAWOS is a free research dataset that copies the Jira data, including the full change log, of 44 open-source software projects (published by Tawosi and colleagues in 2022). We kept the eight projects that ran at least 30 sprints, so each has enough history to learn from: Apache Mesos, Appcelerator Studio, Atlassian Confluence Cloud and Server, LSST Data Management, Mule, Titanium SDK and Titanium Mobile.

4 · Rebuilding day one

A Jira export is not a snapshot of planning day

In short

Today's Jira export shows the sprint as it ended, not as it began. We rewind the change log to rebuild each sprint's first morning, and we log every place where Jira recorded nothing and we had to fall back on today's value.

This is the core idea of the whole project. When you download Jira data today, each issue shows the sprint it is in now and its estimate now. But during a sprint, issues get added, moved out to a later sprint, and re-estimated. A model trained on today's download is told things the team did not know when the sprint began, and so it looks more accurate than it really is.

The fix is to play the change log backwards, like rewinding a video, until we reach the morning the sprint started. Press the button to watch it happen for one sprint.

  2. 2

  4. 4

The rewind is not perfect. For 7.2% of issues we had to assume today's sprint was the original one, and for 45.1% we had to assume today's story points were the original estimate, because Jira never recorded a change. Those guesses could carry a little hindsight. Section 8 shows what happens to the results when we keep only sprints with a complete history: they get somewhat weaker, and the paper says so.
5 · The features

What the model is allowed to know on day one

In short

The model sees about a dozen numbers per sprint: how much was committed, how long the sprint is, how the team did in its recent sprints, how busy it already is, and which project it is.

A machine-learning model does not read Jira screens. For every sprint it is given a short list of numbers, called features, and it learns which numbers go with sprints that closed late or left work unfinished. Some features are copied straight from the sprint (how many issues it has). Most are computed from the team's earlier sprints (how much it usually finishes). All of them are taken from the rebuilt day-one state of section 4, so each one is something the team could have known on the morning the sprint started.

The features, and which models use them

The project has two versions of the feature list. Phase 1 used the first version (called legacy in the paper). Phase 2, Phase 3 and every headline result in the paper use the corrected version (called strict). Section 7 explains what was wrong with the first one.

Which model gets which features

  • Scope-only baseline: just the first three (committed issues, committed story points, sprint length). It tests whether the size of the commitment alone explains the outcome.
  • Logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting: the whole list for their phase (legacy in Phase 1, strict from Phase 2 on). When a model is tested on a project it never trained on (section 8 explains this test), the project feature is removed, because the model cannot know anything about that project.
  • MariaDB outside check: the strict list without the two story-point features, because MariaDB records no story points.
  • Phase 3 in-sprint models: the strict list plus a "progress" group measured during the sprint (share done so far, issues remaining, issues added or removed since the start, time left). Section 10 lists them.

Example: one sprint turned into features

A made-up sprint to show what the numbers look like. Not real data.

On the morning it starts, a two-week Mule sprint has 14 issues worth 31 points. One other Mule sprint is still running. Over the team's last three finished sprints it finished about 10 issues each time out of about 14 committed (72%), left 4 unfinished last time, and closed two of those three sprints late, on average 2.1 days late.

So the strict feature list for this sprint reads: committed issues 14, points 31, length 14 days, other sprints open 1, project Mule, completion ratio 0.72, last carryover 4, points delivered 22.3, history available 3, issues completed 10.3, issues committed 13.7, scope to capacity 1.36 (14 ÷ 10.3), days late 2.1, share late 0.67. The model turns those numbers into one risk score.

Three things to notice

  • Two kinds of feature. The first three describe the plan the team made for this sprint (how much, how long). The rest describe the team itself (how it usually performs). Section 9 shows which kind matters for which outcome.
  • Nothing from inside the sprint. No burndown, no issues done so far. Those come in Phase 3, as a separate "progress" group, and are only used at in-sprint checkpoints.
  • Story points are weak in this data. 73.5% of issues have none, so the point features often read zero. The MariaDB check drops them entirely.
Labels, the thing being predicted, are never features. Spillover (any committed issue unresolved at the planned end) and late closure (closed more than a day after the planned end) are computed from what happened afterwards, and the model only sees them during training.
6 · Reading the scores

What a score of 0.75 means

In short

Most results on this page are a score between 0.5 and 1.0. Higher means the model is better at telling risky sprints from safe ones; 0.5 is no better than a coin toss. (The few results measured differently say so where they appear.)

Each model gives every sprint a risk score. To judge the model, we take the sprints it was tested on and ask one question: did it give the risky sprints higher scores than the safe ones?

A tiny example

SprintWhat really happenedModel's risk score
Aclosed late0.8
Bclosed on time0.3

The late sprint got the higher score, so the model got this pair right. Now repeat that check for every possible pair of one late and one on-time sprint. The share of pairs it gets right is the score. Its full name is ROC-AUC, usually shortened to AUC.

How to read the numbers

0.5coin toss: the model has learned nothing useful
0.70 to 0.85useful, with clear mistakes
0.85+strong
1.0perfect

The same score is used for both outcomes. A late-closure model is scored on late versus on-time sprints; a spillover model on sprints that left work unfinished versus sprints that finished everything.

Some results also show a likely range, for example "0.885 (likely range 0.818 to 0.922)". That means: with a different mix of projects, the score would most likely land somewhere in that range. A wide range means the result is less certain.

7 · Overlapping sprints

"Previous" does not mean "finished"

In short

In these projects several sprints run at the same time, so the sprints just before a new one are often still running when it starts. Phase 1 used their final results anyway, which on that first day did not exist yet. Phase 2 fixed the rule so only sprints that had really finished are used.

Section 5 listed features such as "how did the team do in its last three sprints?" That sounds safe: surely the sprints before this one are finished? In these projects, often not. Many projects run two or more sprints at the same time, for different sub-teams, so a new sprint usually starts while earlier ones are still going. In 71% of our sprints, the sprint just before had not finished yet when the new one started.

Phase 1 rule (legacy)

"Use the three sprints that started most recently before this one."

For Sprint 12 that is Sprints 9, 10 and 11. But Sprints 10 and 11 are still running on day 30. Their results are only known later, so on Sprint 12's first day those numbers did not exist. The feature uses information from the future.

Phase 2 rule (strict)

"Use the three most recent sprints that had finished before this one started."

For Sprint 12 that is Sprints 7, 8 and 9. It skips 10 and 11 and goes further back. Every number it uses was really known on day 30. No information from the future.

How could Phase 1 use results that did not exist yet?

Because the data is historical. It was downloaded years later, when every sprint's final result was already recorded. Phase 1's code simply took the final results of the three sprints that started before, without checking whether they had finished by the time the new sprint began. A real team on day 30 could never have had those numbers. The same applies to two Phase 1 features that use only the single sprint just before (its carryover and completion ratio): here that is Sprint 11, which is still running.

What the change did to the late-closure scores

Phase 1 features (legacy) Phase 2 features (strict)

Higher is better (0.5 is a coin toss; see section 6). "Unseen project" means the model was tested on a project it never trained on; "later sprints" means it was trained on a project's earlier sprints and tested on its later ones (section 8 explains both).

Every model scored higher with the strict features, most of all on unseen projects (logistic regression 0.542 to 0.629). That can seem odd, since the fix takes information away. The reason is that Phase 2 did two things at once: it fixed the rule above, and it added features Phase 1 did not have (the team's past lateness, its usual capacity, and how many sprints were already open; section 5 lists them). Past lateness is the strongest signal for late closure (section 9). So the higher scores mostly come from the new features, and the fixed rule makes sure none of them uses the future. The paper's main numbers, such as 0.751, all use the strict features.

8 · Phase 1

Predicting on day one: what the models learned

In short

Spillover depends on how much the team took on: three simple numbers about the sprint's size give the best ranking (0.885). Late closure depends on team habits: the sprint's size tells you almost nothing, while the team's history gets to 0.751.

With the features from section 5, Phase 1 asks the first question: using only what was known on day one, can a model rank sprints by risk better than a simple rule?

Which numbers are shown here. Phase 1 first ran with the legacy features. After the overlapping-sprint flaw (section 7) was found in Phase 2, the same day-one models were rerun with the corrected strict features, and those are the numbers below and in the paper. For comparison, Phase 1's original legacy features gave late closure 0.705 (random forest).

What is being compared

Each predictor below is built twice: once to predict spillover and once to predict late closure. The first two are simple rules, not real models; a model is only worth having if it beats them.

The empty-sprint trap

As section 3 explained, about a third of sprints (911) had no issues in them on day one, and they are left out of the spillover scores. Here is why that matters. An empty sprint cannot spill over, and the model easily learns "no issues means no spillover", so it gets all of them right for free. That makes the score look much better than it is. With empty sprints included, the random forest scores 0.986; on sprints that actually had work, the same model scores 0.852. So every spillover number on this page, and in the paper, counts only sprints with work in them (called nonempty sprints).

9 · Phase 2

Explaining each outcome, and predicting how much

In short

We measured which features drive each prediction: workload for spillover, and past lateness and the project for late closure. We also tried predicting amounts (how many issues get done, how late); that only works well for issues done.

Phase 2 asked two more questions. First, why does a model call a sprint risky? To answer that we used Shapley values, a method borrowed from game theory. Think of the features as players on a team and the risk score as the team's result: Shapley values work out how much each player contributed, by trying the model with every possible combination of features switched on and off. That is slow, so it was done on a random sample of 150 test sprints, using seven groups of features rather than each feature alone. Second, can the models predict amounts instead of yes or no: how many issues get finished, what share of the work is left over, and how many days late the sprint closes?

Each bar shows how much of the model's decision comes from that group of features. Spillover is about workload: the size of this sprint (41% of the decision) and the team's usual capacity (37%) together make up most of it. Late closure is about habit: whether the team was late before (38%) and which project it is (22%) matter most; the amount of work only 9%.

Numeric targets

  • Issues finished: a simple sum does almost as well as any model. Take the committed issues and multiply by the share the team usually finishes; that is off by 2.1 issues on average. The best model is off by 1.96.
  • Share of work left over (called carry share in the paper): almost impossible to predict on day one. The best model is only slightly better than always guessing the typical value.
  • Days late: the best model is off by 2.48 days on average, against 2.66 for always guessing the typical value. A small gain.

The advisor, and what it is not

Phase 2 packages the trained models into a small advisor. Give it a planned sprint and it returns: the chance of spillover and of late closure; the expected number of issues finished, with a range that should contain the real number 8 times out of 10; and a "what if" table showing how the predictions change if you commit to more or fewer issues. The ranges work well overall (they contain the real number 84% of the time), but for big sprints of 13 or more issues they are too narrow (only 70%).

The what-if table is not advice. It tempts you to read "commit fewer issues and you will spill less". But when we looked at what really happened, the most overloaded quarter of sprints left only 3.2 percentage points more work unfinished than the lightest quarter, too small to be sure it is real. This does not contradict "spillover is about workload". Spillover only asks whether at least one issue is left over, and a bigger sprint simply has more chances for that to happen. The share of work left over barely changes with load. And a model noticing that two things go together does not mean changing one will change the other. The paper repeats this so nobody uses the advisor to cut sprint plans.
10 · Phase 3

In-sprint early warning: does watching help?

In short

Once the sprint is running, progress information makes spillover much easier to predict (0.83 to 0.96 by mid-sprint). It does nothing for late closure, which is about when someone clicks the button, not about the work.

Phases 1 and 2 predict on day one. Phase 3 asks: what if we check again while the sprint is running? We rebuild the sprint at four checkpoints, a quarter, half, three quarters and all of the way through its planned length, using the same rewind method. At each checkpoint the model gets the day-one features plus a set of progress features: how many committed issues are done, how many remain, whether work is behind schedule (share done compared with share of time used), how fast the team is finishing issues, and how many issues were added or removed since the start. The day-one points here are gradient boosting with the strict features, scored only on sprints still open at the first checkpoint, so spillover starts at 0.830 rather than section 8's 0.885 (that was the size-only model). Spillover (orange) becomes much easier to predict once the sprint is running: 0.830 on day one, 0.926 a quarter of the way in, 0.957 at halfway. Late closure (blue) stays flat. Whether someone presses "Complete sprint" on time does not depend on how the work is going.

For spillover it scores 0.667, 0.767 and 0.802 at the 25%, 50% and 75% checkpoints, well below the model, which also knows how much time is left, how big the sprint is and how the team usually performs. For predicting how much work will be left over, though, the simple rule is as good as the model from halfway on.

11 · Leakage case studies

Two mistakes we reproduced on purpose

In short

We made two common mistakes on purpose to show how much they inflate results. Each makes one outcome look far better than it is and barely changes the other, so each outcome has to be checked on its own.

Leakage means information from after the prediction moment sneaking into a model. It makes results look better than they will be in real use. To show how big the effect can be, we deliberately made two mistakes that are common in published work and measured how much they inflate the score. Here the score is not AUC but F1, the score most published studies use. F1 judges the model's final yes/no calls: it balances how many risky sprints are caught against how many false alarms are raised (1.0 is perfect). It is easy to fool. For spillover, where 94% of test sprints spill, a rule that says "yes" to every sprint scores F1 0.971 without looking at anything. That is why the rest of this page uses AUC, and why F1 is the right score to show how big these mistakes look in published work.

done correctly with the mistake

Mistake 1: using an answer as a clue. Give the model the share of work the sprint finished. That is only known after the sprint ends, and it practically is the spillover answer. F1 jumps from 0.756 to 0.991, a near-perfect score that would vanish in real use. Mistake 2: mixing test data into training. SMOTE is a common technique that creates extra, made-up examples of the rarer outcome. If you run it before separating training and test sprints, the made-up examples are partly copied from test sprints, so the model has effectively seen the test. Late-closure F1 rises from 0.457 to 0.646. Notice that each mistake mainly inflates one outcome. Mistake 1 barely moves late closure (0.463 to 0.473, +0.01), because finishing the work is not what decides when someone closes the sprint. Mistake 2 leaves spillover unchanged (0.972), because with 94% of sprints spilling there is almost nothing rare to make up. That is why leakage has to be checked separately for each thing you predict.

12 · Across projects

Leave one project out

In short

When a model is tested on a project it never trained on, the score depends far more on which project it is than on which model is used. Results describe these eight projects, not every team.

Section 8 introduced the two tests. Test 1 (the time split) asks whether a model trained on a project's earlier sprints works on its later ones. Test 2 (leave one project out) asks a harder question: does a model trained on seven projects work on the eighth, a project it has never seen? The chart shows the range of scores across the eight projects.

late closure spillover (sprints with work)

Each line runs from the worst project to the best; the hollow dot is the result for all eight together. Late closure ranges from 0.531 (Titanium Mobile, barely better than a coin toss) to 0.780 (Appcelerator Studio). Spillover ranges from 0.706 (Confluence Cloud, where 267 of its 464 sprints were empty, leaving only 197 to test on) to 0.950. So which project you test on matters more than which model you use. Teams also change over time: Titanium SDK closed 71% of its sprints late in its early years and only 33% later on, so a model trained on the early years is partly out of date.

13 · Outside check

Does it hold on a Jira the model never saw?

In short

Run once, unchanged, on two MariaDB projects the model never saw, late closure still ranks at 0.71. Spillover could not be tested there because nearly every MariaDB sprint spills.

Every test so far used projects from the same dataset, TAWOS. A fair question is whether the results only hold there. So we took two projects from a completely different source: MariaDB, a popular open-source database whose Jira is public. Its MaxScale and ColumnStore projects give 391 sprints from 2016 to 2026. We downloaded them, put them in the same format as TAWOS, and ran the code exactly as it was, without changing or re-tuning anything. One adjustment was unavoidable: MariaDB does not use story points, so the two story-point features were left out, both when training on TAWOS and when testing on MariaDB. The same removal rules as section 3 were applied, plus one more: 5 sprints that belonged to other MariaDB teams' boards were dropped, because most of their work was outside these two projects and could not be seen.

Late closure on MariaDB, 391 sprints, 106 late

late closure score - - - dashed line: 0.5, a coin toss

The two middle bars are the key result. Models trained only on TAWOS, knowing nothing about MariaDB, rank MariaDB's late sprints well: gradient boosting scores 0.710 (likely range 0.637 to 0.773) and the random forest 0.716. That is clearly better than the amount of work alone (0.576), and in the same zone as inside TAWOS: 0.642 when testing on an unseen TAWOS project (section 7, random forest) and 0.751 on later sprints of a known project (section 8). So the late-closure finding is not a quirk of one dataset.

Two limits. First, the two MariaDB projects are very different (one closes 54% of its sprints late, the other 15%), so simply knowing which project a sprint belongs to already scores 0.745, about as well as the best model trained inside MariaDB (the right two bars). That rule needs MariaDB's own past late rates; the TAWOS-trained models never had them, which is what makes their 0.71 meaningful. Second, spillover could not be tested: 99% of MariaDB sprints left something unfinished, so there were only three sprints that did not spill, too few to measure anything.
Also confirmed on MariaDB: watching the sprint's progress still does not help predict late closure (the halfway checkpoint adds only 0.019, too small to be real). And only 10% of MariaDB sprints were empty, against 35% in TAWOS, so the empty-sprint trap from section 8 depends on the data and must be checked each time.

Phase 4 (next section) widens this outside check from two projects to ten, by adding eight MongoDB projects.

14 · Phase 4

Phase 4: testing it the way a reviewer asked

In short

After Phase 3, a professor reviewed the work and asked for tougher testing: repeat every split and report the spread, draw ROC curves with uncertainty, and test on about ten projects the model never saw. Phase 4 does all three. Shuffling sprints makes late closure look better than it really is. On ten new projects late closure still works, but weakly. Spillover still comes down to how much work was committed.

Phases 1 to 3 were not changed. Phase 4 takes their models and features and tests them again, one experiment at a time. Here is what the professor asked for and what we did.

SuggestionWhat Phase 4 does
Shuffle the sprints, split 70/30, repeat 5 times, report mean ± standard deviationPart 1, "Random 70/30"
Random cross-validationPart 1, "Random 5-fold CV", repeated 5 times
Show ROC curves, one for late closure and one for spillover, as a rangePart 2

First, four new words

So far every score on this page was an AUC, which only asks whether risky sprints get higher scores than safe ones. A real tool has to say yes or no. To do that you pick a threshold: every sprint scored above it is flagged as risky. Then three numbers describe the result:

  • Sensitivity: of the sprints that really ran into trouble, how many were flagged? (Missed ones are the cost.)
  • Specificity: of the sprints that went fine, how many were left alone? (False alarms are the cost.)
  • F1: one number that balances catching the risky sprints against raising false alarms.

A strict threshold flags few sprints: high specificity, low sensitivity. A lenient one does the opposite. We set the threshold from the training sprints only, at the point where sensitivity plus specificity is highest, and never adjusted it on the sprints being tested.

Part 1: repeat every split five times

One train/test split gives one number, and you can't tell how lucky it was. So every test was run five times, three different ways:

  • Random 70/30: shuffle all sprints, train on 70%, test on 30%, with a different shuffle each time. This is what the professor asked for.
  • Random 5-fold CV: shuffle, cut into five parts, test on each part in turn, and repeat with five different shuffles.
  • Time order: for each project, keep the sprints in order. Train on the first 40, 50, 60, 70 or 80% and test on the next 20%. The model never learns from a sprint that came after the ones it is tested on, so this keeps the no-hindsight rule from section 1.
late closure spillover (sprints with work) whisker: ± 1 standard deviation over 5 repeats

Gradient boosting, average AUC over 5 repeats. For late closure, shuffling gives 0.765 ± 0.017 and 0.754 ± 0.005, but keeping time order gives 0.696 ± 0.054. Shuffling lets the model learn a team's later habits and then test on its earlier sprints, which can never happen in real use, so it flatters the result. For spillover all three ways agree (0.81 to 0.84), and the amount-of-work baseline still matches the learned models, as in section 8.

Why time order scores lower: part of the gap is simply less training data. Its five repeats learn from 40% up to 80% of each project's history, and the score climbs with it: 0.62, 0.66, 0.71, 0.75, 0.74. With 70% or more, it is back at the 0.751 of section 8. So the honest headline stays the time-ordered number, and the shuffled numbers are the optimistic comparison.

Part 2: ROC curves, drawn as a range

An ROC curve shows every possible threshold at once. Start strict and slowly lower the threshold. Each step flags more sprints: the curve moves up when it catches another risky sprint (sensitivity) and right when it raises another false alarm (1 minus specificity). A model that guesses runs along the diagonal. A good one rises steeply on the left. The AUC is the area under this curve. The shaded band shows how much the curve moves, so you see a range instead of a single line.

Part 3: ten projects the model never saw

Section 13 tested two MariaDB projects. The professor asked for about ten, so we added eight more from MongoDB, another open-source database company with a public Jira: Compass, WiredTiger, the Shell, the Database Tools, and the Java, C, C++ and PHP drivers. We downloaded 47,038 issues, kept the 17,773 that were ever in a sprint, and converted them to the TAWOS format with the same rules as MariaDB. The models were trained once on all of TAWOS and then tested on each project on its own, with no project name and no story points. When a project had fewer than 5 sprints on one side (for example, almost every sprint spilled over), the scores could not be measured and are marked as such.

The threshold does not travel. Sensitivity swings from 0.11 to 0.86 between projects, because late-closure rates differ so much (7% of Compass sprints close late, 54% of ColumnStore's). A threshold that suits TAWOS suits some teams and not others. A real tool would need a short calibration on each new team.

15 · What it means

A protocol more than a tool

In short

The contribution is a careful way of working with Jira history, not a tool to deploy. Six habits carry over to any similar study.

The main result is not a product you can switch on tomorrow. It is a careful method for using Jira history, and six lessons that apply to anyone, researcher or team, who tries to learn from it.

  1. 1
    Rebuild the sprint-start state from the change log. A present-day export carries hindsight.
  2. 2
    Leave out empty sprints when measuring spillover, or the score is inflated by easy answers.
  3. 3
    Watch out for sprints that overlap. Only use history from sprints that had already finished.
  4. 4
    Check for leakage separately for each outcome. The same mistake inflated one outcome by 0.24 and the other by only 0.01.
  5. 5
    Keep time order when you test, and repeat the test. Shuffling sprints made late closure look 0.07 better than it is (section 14).
  6. 6
    Test on teams the model never saw, and expect to re-set the threshold. The ranking carried over to ten outside projects; the yes/no cut-off did not.

What a team can actually do with this

At planning, the scores can put sprints in order of risk, so a team or Scrum Master knows which ones to keep an eye on. Treat them as an order, not as exact chances ("70% likely"), because the probabilities are not reliable across projects. During the sprint, the quarter-way and halfway checks spot likely spillover earlier and better than just counting open issues. Late closure comes from team habits, so the way to improve it is a team conversation about closing sprints on time, not a prediction.

Limits

  • Jira's completion timestamp marks when a sprint was closed, not when its work was done.
  • What Jira never recorded cannot be recovered, and the gaps in story-point history limit how far the spillover result can be trusted.
  • Eight projects is a small sample, so many of the likely ranges are wide.
  • Every project is open-source. The outside checks add two sources (MariaDB and MongoDB) and ten projects, but spillover could be measured in only four of them, and the in-sprint models of Phase 3 have had only one outside test (late closure at halfway, on MariaDB).

17 · Glossary

Terms used above

Scrum
A way of running software work in short fixed cycles with a committed plan for each.
Backlog and issue
The backlog is the team's list of work; each item is an issue (a story, task or bug).
Model
A formula learned from past sprints that turns a sprint's features into a risk score.
Feature
One of the numbers the model is given about a sprint. Section 5 lists them.
Label
The outcome being predicted (spilled over, closed late). Known only after the sprint ends.
Training and test sprints
Sprints the model learns from, and sprints it is scored on but never saw while learning.
Baseline
A simple rule (always yes, the project's own rate, scope only) that a learned model must beat to be worth anything.
Logistic regression
A straight-line model: each feature adds or subtracts a fixed amount from the score.
Random forest and gradient boosting
Models built from many small decision trees; the forest lets trees vote, boosting builds each tree to fix the last one's mistakes.
Sprint
A fixed time box, usually one to four weeks, in which a Scrum team commits to a set of issues.
Committed scope
The issues (and their story points) attached to a sprint on its first day, as rebuilt from the change log.
Story points
A team's relative estimate of an issue's size. 73.5% of TAWOS issues have none; MariaDB uses none at all.
Change log
Jira's record of every field change on an issue, with a timestamp. It is what lets us rewind.
Fallback
Using today's value of a field because Jira never recorded a change to it, so there is nothing to rewind. Noted for every issue, because it can carry hindsight.
ROC-AUC (or just AUC)
Pick one sprint that ran over and one that did not: how often does the model give the first a higher risk score? 0.5 is a coin toss, 1.0 is perfect. Section 6 explains it.
Threshold
The cut-off that turns a risk score into a yes/no call: sprints scored above it are flagged. In Phase 4 it is set on the training sprints only.
Sensitivity and specificity
Sensitivity: of the sprints that really ran into trouble, the share that were flagged. Specificity: of the sprints that went fine, the share that were left alone.
ROC curve
A plot of sensitivity against false alarms (1 minus specificity) for every possible threshold. The area under it is the AUC.
Shuffled split and cross-validation
Splitting sprints into training and test at random, ignoring time. Cross-validation cuts the data into parts and tests on each part in turn. Both can let a model learn from a team's future.
Mean ± standard deviation
The average of several repeats of a test, and how much the repeats differ from that average.
F1
A score for yes/no calls that balances catching the risky sprints against raising false alarms. 1.0 is perfect. Easily inflated when nearly every sprint is risky.
Time-ordered split
Train on each project's first 70% of sprints, test on its last 30%. Never trains on the future.
LOPO
Leave-one-project-out: train on seven projects, test on the eighth, repeated for each.
Likely range (95% interval)
The range a score would fall in 95 times out of 100 if the study were repeated with a different mix of projects. Computed by re-scoring the models 2,000 times on reshuffled projects (a "cluster bootstrap"). For MariaDB, with only two projects, sprints are reshuffled instead.
Shapley values
A fair way to split a model's prediction among its inputs, computed exactly here over seven feature groups.
Leakage
Any way information from after the prediction moment reaches the model. The project's central concern.
Strict vs legacy features
Two versions of the team-history features. Legacy (Phase 1) used the most recent sprints even if they were still running; strict (Phase 2 on) uses only sprints that had already finished.
Spillover and late closure
The two outcomes predicted: work left unfinished at the planned end, and the sprint closed in Jira more than a day late.
Nonempty sprint
A sprint that had at least one issue in it on day one. Spillover is only measured on these.
Checkpoint
A moment inside a running sprint (25%, 50%, 75% or 100% of its planned length) at which Phase 3 makes a new prediction.
SMOTE
A technique that creates made-up examples of the rarer outcome to balance the training data. Safe only if done after the test sprints are set aside.
