1 · Start here

If you have never seen this project, read this first

In short

We taught a computer to look at a software team's plan on the first day of a sprint and say how likely the sprint is to run over. The hard part was making sure the computer only saw what the team could have seen that morning.

How to read this page

Every section opens with an "In short" box. Read only those and you have the whole story in five minutes. The full page takes about half an hour. Figures with a Play, Draw or Show button are animated: press them. Any word you don't know is in the glossary at the end.

Scrum in one minute

A Scrum team keeps a backlog, a list of work items called issues. Every one to four weeks it starts a sprint: at a planning meeting it picks the issues it commits to finish and, often, gives each a size estimate in story points. Then it works. At the planned end the team closes the sprint and anything unfinished rolls over.

Jira in one minute

Jira is the tool most teams use to run this. Each issue has fields (status, sprint, story points, assignee). Jira also keeps a change log: every time a field changes, it records from what, to what, and when. That change log is the raw material of this whole project.

What "predicting" means here

A model is a formula learned from past sprints. You give it a handful of numbers about a new sprint (its features) and it returns a risk score. To learn the formula the model is shown old sprints together with how they ended (their labels). To check it honestly, it is then tested on sprints it never saw during learning.

Jira change logevery field change, with a timestamp
Day-one statethe board as it was when each sprint began
Featuresa dozen numbers per sprint
Modela formula learned from older sprints
Risk scorechecked against what really happened
The one rule that runs through everything: no hindsight. Nothing recorded after the moment of prediction may reach the model. Most of this project is about the ways that rule is easy to break by accident, and what breaking it costs.
2 · The question

Two ways a sprint misses its boundary

In short

A sprint can fail in two recorded ways: work left unfinished (spillover) or the sprint closed late in Jira (late closure). We ask whether either can be predicted on day one, and whether watching the sprint helps.

Scrum teams plan a sprint, commit to a set of issues, and run for one to four weeks. Jira records two things when that goes wrong, and neither requires anyone's opinion.

Spillover

At least one issue that was committed at the start is still unresolved at the planned end. Work rolled into the next sprint.

Late closure

Jira's "complete sprint" timestamp lands more than one day after the planned end. An administrative event, not a verdict on the team.

The research question is simple to state: can either outcome be ranked from what was known on the first day of the sprint, and does watching the sprint unfold add anything? The rule that makes it hard: every feature must be rebuilt only from events timestamped at or before the moment of prediction. No hindsight.

Both labels are very common. 38.7% of modelled sprints closed late, and 93.1% of sprints with committed work spilled over. So the useful question for spillover is not "will any work spill?" but "which sprints carry the most unfinished work?"
3 · The data

TAWOS, and how 2,657 sprint rows became 2,602

In short

Eight open-source projects from the public TAWOS dataset, 2,602 sprints after removing ones that were still running, broken, or had no history. A third of them committed no work at all.

TAWOS is a public dataset of 44 open-source Jira projects with their full issue history (Tawosi et al., MSR 2022). We kept the eight projects with at least 30 sprint records: Apache Mesos, Appcelerator Studio, Atlassian Confluence Cloud and Server, LSST Data Management, Mule, Titanium SDK and Titanium Mobile.

kept removed

Three cuts, each for a stated reason. Sprints still open have no outcome yet; three closed rows end before they begin; each project's first sprint has no history to learn from. A third of what remains committed no issues at all, which matters later.

4 · Rebuilding day one

A Jira export is not a snapshot of planning day

In short

Today's Jira export shows the sprint as it ended, not as it began. We rewind the change log to rebuild each sprint's first morning, and we log every place where Jira recorded nothing and we had to fall back on today's value.

This is the core idea of the whole project. When you export Jira today, each issue carries its current sprint and its current estimate. But issues get added mid-sprint, pulled out, re-estimated. If you train a model on today's export, you feed it information the team did not have when the sprint began, and the model looks better than it is.

The fix is to replay the change log backwards. Press play to watch one sprint being rewound from today's export to the state on its first morning.

Today Sprint 12 shows five issues: A, B, C (5 points), D, E. The change log says otherwise.

  1. 1
    Start from the latest recorded state of each issue's Sprint and Story Points fields.
  2. 2
    Walk the change events backwards past the sprint's start timestamp, undoing each one.
  3. 3
    Count an issue as committed only if, at that moment, it existed, belonged to the sprint, and was not yet resolved.
  4. 4
    Log every fallback. When an issue has no recorded change for a field, we use the static value and record that we did.
The data has a ceiling. 7.2% of commitments lean on the static sprint link and 45.1% on static story points, because Jira never recorded a change. On the 110 test sprints whose scope has fully recorded history, the headline spillover score drops from 0.885 to 0.772. The paper reports that plainly instead of hiding it.
5 · The features

What the model is allowed to know on day one

In short

The model sees about a dozen numbers per sprint: how much was committed, how long the sprint is, how the team did in its recent sprints, how busy it already is, and which project it is.

A machine-learning model does not read Jira. It reads a short list of numbers per sprint, called features. Everything below is computed from the rebuilt day-one state, and the rule from section 4 applies to each one: it must be knowable on the morning the sprint starts.

Three things the feature list tells you

  • Scope is what the team chose. Everything else is what the team is. The models have to work out which matters for which outcome; Phase 2 answers that.
  • Nothing from inside the sprint. No burndown, no issues done so far. Those come in Phase 3, as a separate "progress" group, and are only used at in-sprint checkpoints.
  • Story points are weak in this data. 73.5% of issues have none, so the point features often read zero. The MariaDB check later drops them entirely.
Labels, the thing being predicted, are never features. Spillover (any committed issue unresolved at the planned end) and late closure (closed more than a day after the planned end) are computed from what happened afterwards, and the model only sees them during training.
6 · Scoring a model

How we tell a good model from a lucky one

In short

Models are compared with one number, ROC-AUC, which asks how well the model ranks risky sprints above safe ones. They are always tested on sprints they never trained on, and every headline number comes with a comparison to a simple rule.

ROC-AUC, the headline number

Pick one sprint that did run over and one that did not, at random. Did the model give the risky one the higher score? Do that for every possible pair and count the share of times it got the order right. That share is the ROC-AUC. 0.5 means the model is no better than a coin toss, 1.0 means it never gets a pair wrong. Try it below.

Twelve example sprints, sorted by the model's risk score
closed late closed on time

Pairs drawn: 0. The exact AUC of this little example is 0.806: of the 36 possible pairs, the model orders 29 correctly. After enough random pairs the tally settles near that.

Why not accuracy or F1? Because the labels are lopsided. 93% of sprints with work spill over, so a model that says "yes" to everything already scores F1 0.971 on spillover. AUC is unmoved by that; it only cares about the ordering. F1 still appears in the leakage section, where its sensitivity is the point.

How a model is tested without cheating

Time-ordered split

Leave one project out (LOPO)

Uncertainty comes from a cluster bootstrap: the eight projects are resampled 2,000 times and the model is re-scored each time, which gives a 95% interval for every difference between two models. Eight projects make those intervals wide, and the paper says so.

7 · The second leak

Projects run several sprints at once

In short

Teams run sprints in parallel, so "the previous sprint" was often still open when the next began. Features that summarised it were reading the future. Fixing that made the models better, not worse.

A natural feature is "how did the previous sprint go?" But in 71% of our sprints, the previous sprint had not reached its planned end when the next one started. Its outcome was not known yet. A feature that reads it is reading the future.

Two overlapping sprints of one project

Legacy history features (Phase 1) summarised the immediately preceding sprints whether or not they had finished. Strict features (Phase 2) use only sprints whose outcome was already known at the start. Both are reported; the paper's headline numbers use strict.

What fixing it changed: late-closure AUC, legacy vs strict

legacy strict

Across projects (leave-one-project-out) the lift is largest: logistic regression 0.542 to 0.629, random forest 0.600 to 0.642. The legacy features were less informative than they looked, and had no business being called "known at planning time".

8 · Phase 1

Planning-time prediction: what the models learned

In short

Spillover is about how much the team took on: three numbers about scope rank sprints at AUC 0.885 and nothing richer beats them. Late closure is about team habits: scope alone is a coin toss, and history gets to 0.751.

With the features from section 5 and the scoring rules from section 6, Phase 1 asks the first question: using only what was known at planning time, can a model rank sprints by risk better than a simple rule?

Time-ordered split: train on each project's first 70% of sprints, test on the rest

late closure (786 test sprints) spillover, nonempty sprints (511 test sprints)

The two outcomes behave in opposite ways. For spillover, a three-variable logistic regression on scope alone (0.885) beats every richer model. For late closure, scope alone is a coin toss (0.530) and gradient boosting on team history reaches 0.751.

The zero-scope trap

A third of sprints committed nothing. They cannot spill over, so a model gets them right for free. Keep them in and the spillover forest scores 0.986; score only sprints with real work and the same model scores 0.852. A study that keeps them looks about 0.13 AUC stronger than it is. Every spillover number in the paper is on nonempty sprints.

9 · Phase 2

Explaining each outcome, and predicting how much

In short

Shapley values show which features drive each prediction: scope and capacity for spillover, past lateness and project identity for late closure. Predicting exact numbers works for issues completed and little else.

Phase 2 asked two more things. First, why does a model score a sprint as risky? We computed exact Shapley values over seven feature groups: for each sprint, every combination of groups is scored and the credit is shared out fairly. Second, can we predict numbers rather than yes/no: how many issues get done, what share carries over, how many days late?

Share of model attribution by feature group (random forest, strict features, 150 test sprints)

late closure
spillover

Spillover is a work outcome: how much was taken on, against how much the team usually clears. Late closure is a habit outcome: past lateness and project identity carry 60% of it, and committed scope only 9%.

Numeric targets

Issues completed is mostly arithmetic: multiply committed issues by the team's recent completion rate and you are within 2.1 issues on average; the best learned model manages 1.96. Carry share is nearly unpredictable at planning time. Days late improves only from 2.66 to 2.48 days of error.

The advisor, and what it is not

Phase 2 wraps the fitted models into an advisor: for a planned sprint it returns both probabilities, the expected issues completed with an 80% interval, and a what-if table across alternative scopes. The intervals cover 84% of outcomes and are a third tighter than the baseline, but they under-cover large sprints (70% coverage at 13 or more issues).

The what-if table is not advice. Sprints planned heaviest relative to capacity carry over only 3.2 percentage points more than the lightest, and the interval crosses zero. A model's expectation says nothing about cause. The paper says this in four places so that nobody deploys it as a scope-cutting tool.
10 · Phase 3

In-sprint early warning: does watching help?

In short

Once the sprint is running, progress information makes spillover much easier to predict (0.83 to 0.96 by mid-sprint). It does nothing for late closure, which is about when someone clicks the button, not about the work.

Phase 3 rebuilds the sprint state at 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% of the planned duration, again only from events timestamped at or before that point: issues done and remaining, the gap between work finished and time elapsed, issues added or dropped since the start. Press play to see how the prediction changes as a sprint runs.

At planning time: spillover AUC 0.830, late closure 0.751
spillover, model with progress late closure, model with progress spillover, learning-free rule (share of work still open)

Spillover becomes much more predictable once the sprint is running: 0.830 at planning, 0.926 a quarter of the way in, 0.957 at halfway. Late closure barely moves. Of 24 paired comparisons with the same model without progress, 22 intervals include zero. Whether somebody clicks "complete sprint" on time is not about how the work is going.

A plain rule, "share of committed work still open", reaches 0.667, 0.767 and 0.802 at the three checkpoints. The model is worth 0.10 to 0.26 AUC over it, because it also uses time pressure, scope and history. For carry share the rule "one minus the share done" is as good as the model from halfway on.

11 · Leakage case studies

Two mistakes we reproduced on purpose

In short

Two common mistakes, made on purpose, inflate results by up to 0.24 F1. Each hits one outcome and barely touches the other, so leakage has to be checked per task.

To show how much hindsight inflates a result, we made two common mistakes on our own data and measured the damage at the fixed threshold.

done correctly with the mistake

Mistake 1: feed in the sprint's own completion ratio, which is only known once the sprint is over. Spillover F1 jumps from 0.756 to 0.991 because the feature restates the label. Mistake 2: run SMOTE oversampling on the whole pool before splitting. Late-closure F1 inflates from 0.457 to 0.646 as synthetic copies of test sprints leak into training. Each mistake hits one label and barely touches the other, so a leakage audit has to be run per task.

12 · Across projects

Leave one project out

In short

Train on seven projects and test on the eighth, and performance swings far more by project than by model. The numbers are evidence about these projects, not a general benchmark.

The time split tests whether a model learned on a project's past works on its future. Leave-one-project-out (LOPO) asks a harder question: train on seven projects, test on the eighth. It is the project, far more than the model, that moves performance.

late closure spillover, nonempty

Late closure runs from 0.531 on Titanium Mobile to 0.780 on Appcelerator Studio (pooled 0.600 with legacy features, 0.642 strict). Spillover from 0.706 on Confluence Cloud, where 267 of 464 sprints commit nothing, to 0.950 on Appcelerator. Concept drift is part of why: Titanium SDK's late rate drops from 71% in training to 33% in test.

13 · Outside check

Does it hold on a Jira the model never saw?

In short

Run once, unchanged, on two MariaDB projects the model never saw, late closure still ranks at 0.71. Spillover could not be tested there because nearly every MariaDB sprint spills.

LOPO never leaves TAWOS. So on 1 November 2026 we ran the frozen pipeline once on MariaDB's public Jira: the MaxScale and ColumnStore projects, 391 sprints from 2016 to 2026, pulled through the REST API and converted to the TAWOS table layout. No model, label, feature or reconstruction code was changed. MariaDB records no story points, so that run drops the point features on both sides.

Late closure on MariaDB, 391 sprints, 106 late

Trained on TAWOS alone, with no project identity and no story points, gradient boosting ranks MariaDB late closures at 0.710 (interval 0.637 to 0.773) and the random forest at 0.716, against 0.576 for scope alone. That sits between the paper's LOPO (0.642) and time-split (0.751) numbers, so the late-closure result is not a TAWOS artifact.

Two honest caveats. Within MariaDB, the two projects close late at such different rates (54% and 15%) that knowing the project alone scores 0.745, as good as the best model. And spillover cannot be tested there: 99% of MariaDB sprints spill over, leaving three negatives.
Replicated: progress still adds almost nothing for late closure at mid-sprint (+0.019, interval crossing zero), and the zero-scope inflation is smaller because only 10% of MariaDB sprints are empty, against 35% in TAWOS.
14 · What it means

A protocol more than a tool

In short

The contribution is a careful way of working with Jira history, not a tool to deploy. Four habits carry over to any similar study.

The contribution is the way of working with Jira history, not a risk score to deploy. Four lessons travel to any team or researcher doing this kind of work.

  1. 1
    Rebuild the sprint-start state from the change log. A present-day export carries hindsight.
  2. 2
    Drop the sprints that commit nothing before scoring spillover, or your AUC is inflated by free answers.
  3. 3
    Honour parallel-sprint timing. Only use history whose outcome was known when the sprint began.
  4. 4
    Audit for leakage one label at a time. The same mistake costs 0.24 F1 on one outcome and 0.01 on the other.

What a team can actually do with this

At planning, the scores rank sprints by risk, so a team knows which ones to watch; they are rankings, not calibrated odds. During the sprint, the 25% and 50% checkpoints flag likely spillover earlier and better than counting open issues. Late closure is driven by habits, not by the work, so no in-sprint signal helps it.

Limits, in the paper's own words

  • Jira's completion timestamp marks when a sprint was closed, not when its work was done.
  • What was never recorded cannot be recovered; the story-point fallback caps the spillover estimate.
  • Eight projects give wide intervals; the bootstrap over projects is exploratory, not confirmatory.
  • Every project is open-source, and the MariaDB check adds one outside source, two projects, and no test of spillover.

16 · Glossary

Terms used above

Scrum
A way of running software work in short fixed cycles with a committed plan for each.
Backlog and issue
The backlog is the team's list of work; each item is an issue (a story, task or bug).
Model
A formula learned from past sprints that turns a sprint's features into a risk score.
Feature
One of the numbers the model is given about a sprint. Section 5 lists them.
Label
The outcome being predicted (spilled over, closed late). Known only after the sprint ends.
Training and test sprints
Sprints the model learns from, and sprints it is scored on but never saw while learning.
Baseline
A simple rule (always yes, the project's own rate, scope only) that a learned model must beat to be worth anything.
Logistic regression
A straight-line model: each feature adds or subtracts a fixed amount from the score.
Random forest and gradient boosting
Models built from many small decision trees; the forest lets trees vote, boosting builds each tree to fix the last one's mistakes.
Sprint
A fixed time box, usually one to four weeks, in which a Scrum team commits to a set of issues.
Committed scope
The issues (and their story points) attached to a sprint on its first day, as rebuilt from the change log.
Story points
A team's relative estimate of an issue's size. 73.5% of TAWOS issues have none; MariaDB uses none at all.
Change log
Jira's record of every field change on an issue, with a timestamp. It is what lets us rewind.
Static fallback
Using today's value of a field because no change was ever recorded for it. Logged, because it can carry hindsight.
ROC-AUC
How often the model ranks a random positive above a random negative. 0.5 is chance, 1.0 is perfect.
F1
The balance of precision and recall at a fixed threshold. Easily inflated when nearly everything is positive.
Time-ordered split
Train on each project's first 70% of sprints, test on its last 30%. Never trains on the future.
LOPO
Leave-one-project-out: train on seven projects, test on the eighth, repeated for each.
Cluster bootstrap
Resampling the eight projects 2,000 times to put an interval around a difference between models.
Shapley values
A fair way to split a model's prediction among its inputs, computed exactly here over seven feature groups.
Leakage
Any way information from after the prediction moment reaches the model. The project's central concern.
Strict vs legacy history
Strict uses only earlier sprints whose outcome was already known; legacy used the immediately preceding sprints regardless.
