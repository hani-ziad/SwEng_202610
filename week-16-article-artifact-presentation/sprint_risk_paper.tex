\begin{abstract}
Repository data are useful at Sprint Planning only if the features reflect what was known at that time. We examine two repository-derived outcomes in eight TAWOS projects: late sprint closure and unfinished scope that was committed when the sprint began. Sprint membership and story points are reconstructed from Jira change history, with a documented static fallback when no relevant history is recorded. After removing nonclosed and invalid sprint rows and one genuine cold start per project, 2,602 sprints remain. Late closure is only moderately discriminable: random forest reaches 0.705 area under the receiver operating characteristic curve on the main time-ordered split, with a project-cluster 95\% interval of 0.604--0.783. For nonempty sprints, a simple scope-only logistic model reaches 0.885 on this measure, compared with 0.852 for random forest. The simpler model therefore captures most of the useful spillover ranking signal. Provenance matters: when the analysis is restricted to the much smaller subset in which every committed item has observed Sprint and Story Points history, the random-forest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve falls to 0.652 on 110 test sprints. Results are also reported across multiple temporal cut points and held-out projects. The evidence supports cautious retrospective ranking of these two outcomes, not a deployment-ready Scrum decision system.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Sprint Planning is one of the few points in Scrum where the team can still change the amount and shape of the work before execution begins. By the time blockers appear, a burndown stops moving, or unfinished work is repeatedly discussed in the Daily Scrum, the sprint is already in progress. We therefore treat sprint start as a hard cutoff for the prediction data. Anything recorded or changed after that point is not used as if it had been known during planning.

We use two specific outcomes rather than a broad measure of sprint success. A sprint is labeled \emph{late sprint closure} when Jira records completion more than one day after the planned end. \emph{Unfinished committed scope} means that at least one issue present when the sprint started is still unresolved at the planned end. These labels describe repository events only; they should not be read as measures of the team's overall performance.

A final Jira export is not a historical snapshot. Issues move between sprints, estimates change, and some records are created after a sprint has already started. To reduce that timing error, we reconstruct Sprint membership and Story Points from timestamped TAWOS change records. When a field has no relevant history, the static value is used as a fallback and is reported as such. We exclude developer count because assignee-at-start cannot be reconstructed reliably from the available export.

Four questions guide the analysis: (1) how much ranking signal is present for late closure and unfinished committed scope; (2) whether a simple scope model performs as well as more flexible models; (3) how stable the findings are across projects and temporal cut points; and (4) how sensitive the results are to incomplete historical provenance and common leakage mistakes.

\section{Related Work}
Machine learning in Agile software analytics has most often been used for effort estimation, sprint monitoring, and project-risk support. Choetkiertikul et al.~\cite{choetkiertikul2019} estimated story points from Jira text, while Shankar et al.~\cite{shankar2026} compared several predictive models for Agile effort data. P\'erez Castillo et al.~\cite{perezcastillo2024} classified sprint performance using burndown information, which is observed after the sprint has begun. Obike et al.~\cite{obike2025} focused on sprint deliverability with an ensemble method, while Almalki~\cite{almalki2025} examined interpretable AI for Agile project-risk support.

The real question is narrower: did we actually have access to these predictors at the time the sprint began? To check this, we reconstruct time-varying fields as they would have looked at that point, run simple baselines alongside random forest and XGBoost, and evaluate on both later sprints and entirely held-out projects. This matters because it stops the models from getting credit for final-state values they couldn't have known during planning.

Not every issue has a recorded Sprint or Story Points change. When a change is missing, we fall back to the static value and treat it as unchanged. This is an assumption; the data cannot confirm that the field truly stayed constant. Since coverage differs across projects, we report the provenance of the commitments and rerun the spillover analysis on subsets with observed history.

The reconstruction is applied in the same order for every issue. Change events are sorted by issue, timestamp, and change ID. The state begins from the latest recorded value and is rolled backward to each sprint start. A change recorded exactly at the start timestamp is treated as available at the start; only later changes are undone. Multi-sprint Jira values are parsed as sets, and unknown sprint identifiers are ignored if they do not map to a retained sprint.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
\item Load Sprint, Issue, and Change\_Log rows for the eight projects.
\item Remove nonclosed and invalid sprint rows before any labels are created.
\item Build project-specific maps for database sprint IDs and Jira sprint IDs.
\item Reconstruct Sprint membership and Story Points at each sprint's start time.
\item Keep an issue only if it existed by the start time, belonged to that sprint, and had not already been resolved.
\item Aggregate committed issues, committed points, completed issues, delivered points, and carryover.
\item Build labels and historical features, then remove one genuine cold start per project.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Outcomes and features}
Late closure is defined as
\begin{equation}
y^{late}_s = \mathbb{1}[c_s-e_s>1\ \mathrm{day}],
\end{equation}
where $c_s$ is Jira's completion timestamp and $e_s$ is the planned end timestamp. Missing completion dates are never coded as on time; the corresponding nonclosed sprints are excluded before modeling.

Unfinished committed scope is defined as
\begin{equation}
y^{spill}_s = \mathbb{1}[u_s>0],
\end{equation}
where $u_s$ is the number of sprint-start committed issues unresolved by the planned end. The main conditional analysis excludes reconstructed zero-scope sprints because they are negative by construction.

Current-sprint predictors are committed issue count, committed story points, and planned sprint length. Historical predictors summarize recent delivered points, recent completion ratio, prior carryover, and the immediately preceding sprint's completion ratio. Project identity is included only in the full models. The scope-only logistic model uses current scope and sprint length, with no project identity or historical variables.

For the main test, we order each project's sprints chronologically, training on the earlier 70\% and testing on the later 30\%. We also repeat this at 50/50, 60/40, and 80/20 splits. In leave-one-project-out (LOPO) testing, we hold out one entire project from training and test on it separately --- though its earlier sprints still feed the historical features, so this measures how well the model transfers across projects rather than a true cold start on a brand-new project.

For uncertainty on the main split, we resample projects as whole units rather than sampling individual sprint rows. This keeps sprints from the same project together. Feature importance is measured by permuting variables on held-out data rather than using the forest's built-in impurity scores alone.

\subsection{Main test results}
On the main 70/30 split, 246 of 786 test sprints are late. Random forest gives the highest late-closure ROC-AUC, 0.705, with an F1 of 0.562. The scope-only logistic model manages just 0.530 AUC --- barely above chance. This suggests starting scope alone tells us little about whether a sprint will close late, whereas project and historical context contribute at least some useful signal. Even with that extra information, the ranking remains moderate.

For nonempty spillover, 482 of 511 test sprints are positive. Because almost every sprint is positive, an always-positive rule already gets F1=0.971; F1 is therefore not a useful headline by itself. The scope-only logistic model reaches 0.885 ROC-AUC, compared with 0.852 for the full random forest. In this test, most of the ranking information is already in the starting scope, and the larger model does not improve AUC.

\subsection{Uncertainty and alternative time splits}
Project-level resampling gives a 95\% AUC interval of 0.604--0.783 for the late-closure random forest. For nonempty spillover, the interval is 0.820--0.922 for scope-only logistic regression and 0.797--0.914 for random forest. The intervals overlap, so the 0.033 AUC gap on the main split is not strong evidence that one model will always outperform the other.

The forward splits are consistent with that picture. Late-closure random-forest AUC varies from 0.669 at 50/50 to 0.711 at 80/20. For nonempty spillover, random forest stays between 0.846 and 0.856, while scope-only logistic regression ranges from 0.865 to 0.887. The result is therefore not specific to the 70/30 split.

\subsection{Provenance sensitivity}
Our main reconstruction blends observed change history with a static fallback wherever no change is recorded, and that coverage varies a lot by project --- 96.4\% of Apache Mesos commitments have observed Sprint history, for instance, versus just 54.1\% for Confluence Cloud. Story-point history is even sparser.

Table~\ref{tab:prov} narrows the conditional spillover analysis down to subsets with stronger provenance. Requiring observed Sprint history for every committed item cuts the sample to 1,174 modeled sprints (356 in the test set), where scope-only logistic regression still holds at 0.875 AUC and random forest at 0.850. Requiring both Sprint and Story Points history shrinks things further, to just 358 modeled sprints and 110 test sprints. On this stricter subset, scope-only AUC drops to 0.772 and random forest to 0.652. The smaller sample means less stable estimates, but the drop itself is worth taking seriously --- it shows that provenance quality changes how hard the task actually looks.

Reliability bins make clear that these raw probabilities shouldn't be treated as literal risk percentages. That's partly a consequence of how common conditional spillover is and partly a consequence of class weighting. We treat the probability scores mainly as a way to rank sprints, not as calibrated risk estimates.

Two controlled leakage checks show how easily evaluation choices can distort the results. Adding the current sprint's own completion ratio as a feature barely moves late-closure F1 but pushes spillover F1 nearly to 1. Oversampling before the train/test split, meanwhile, substantially inflates late-closure F1 while doing almost nothing to the spillover task, which is already highly imbalanced toward positives. So leakage doesn't inflate results by some fixed amount --- how much damage it does depends on the target and the specific mechanism involved.

\subsection{Outcome-definition sensitivity}
The broad findings are not tied to one exact threshold. For late closure, random-forest AUC is 0.696 with no grace day, 0.705 with the one-day definition, 0.685 with a three-day tolerance, and 0.626 with seven days. In the full spillover population, requiring at least two or three unfinished issues still produces AUCs of 0.984 and 0.980. A story-point completion-ratio label below 0.8 gives 0.977 AUC. These checks do not remove the provenance and prevalence concerns, but they show that the full-population separation is not created by a single arbitrary cutoff.

\subsection{Reconstruction audit}
The exported change log contains 88,364 relevant Sprint and Story Points events. At issue level, history coverage varies substantially: 38.1\% of Titanium Mobile issues have Sprint history, compared with 2.9\% in Confluence Cloud. Commitment-level coverage is higher because committed items are not a random sample of all project issues. Both project coverage and stricter subset results are reported because missing history is not uniform across the data.

Several edge cases are handled explicitly: multi-valued Sprint fields are treated as sets; a historical sprint identifier belonging to a sprint outside the retained subset is ignored rather than forced into a selected sprint; missing story points contribute zero points without removing an otherwise valid commitment; and issues resolved before the sprint starts are excluded from planning scope, since they cannot represent unresolved work at that time.

\section{Discussion}
The clearest finding is not that a complex model wins. It is that the two repository outcomes behave differently. Late closure contains a modest signal that depends on project context and sprint length, and that signal transfers unevenly. Unfinished committed scope is easier to rank, but most of that ranking comes from the amount of work present at sprint start, so a transparent scope-only model earns its place as a real baseline here, not just a formality.

This also narrows what we're actually claiming. The analysis can rank historical sprints under our stated reconstruction assumptions --- it does not show that a Scrum Master should act on any given score, that using the score would improve delivery, or that the model would calibrate well in a different organization. A more sensible next step would be a silent prospective deployment: generate scores without letting them influence team behavior, calibrate locally, and only then consider an actual intervention study.

The provenance sensitivity is equally important. Reconstructing timestamped fields is more defensible than using a static export, but incomplete change history remains an identification problem. The strict observed-history subset is smaller and produces weaker random-forest discrimination. Any use of the main estimate should therefore keep the fallback assumption visible.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\textit{Construct validity.} Jira's completion timestamp marks when the sprint was closed, not necessarily when the work itself was finished. The spillover label is similarly narrow --- it only captures whether at least one item committed at sprint start was still unresolved by the planned end. Neither label should be treated as a stand-in for overall team performance.

\textit{Reconstruction validity.} Missing Sprint or Story Points history is handled with static fallback. The provenance tables and sensitivity analysis show how much the result depends on that choice, but they cannot recover events that were never recorded. Jira use also differs by project.

\textit{Evaluation validity.} Only eight projects are available, so project-level uncertainty is necessarily wide. LOPO retains local historical features from the held-out project and should be interpreted as transfer of the learned rule rather than a complete cold start. Thresholded metrics are affected by prevalence, especially for conditional spillover.

\textit{External validity.} The projects are open-source Jira projects. Enterprise teams may differ in staffing, backlog practice, and administrative use of sprints. Absolute performance should not be transported without local validation.

\balance
\section{Reproducibility and Audit Trail}
The artifact separates data preparation from modeling. Three CSV exports (Sprint, Issue, and Sprint/Story Points rows from Change\_Log) feed the reconstruction, and analysis outputs are saved as CSV or JSON. The principal results are regenerated by \texttt{scripts.run\_baseline}, \texttt{scripts.audit\_commitment\_provenance}, and \texttt{scripts.run\_validation\_sensitivity\_analyses}; label-sensitivity and leakage checks remain separate.

Automated tests cover labels, cold starts, planning snapshots, feature construction, scope decomposition, and leakage cases. A manuscript-number crosswalk links headline statistics to result files. Reproducibility targets the cleaned cohort, split definitions, features, and metrics within ordinary numerical tolerance. The recorded environment was Python 3.13.5, NumPy 2.3.5, pandas 2.2.3, scikit-learn 1.8.0, and XGBoost 3.1.3.

\section{Conclusion}
Reconstructed Jira history contains measurable signal for two sprint-start outcomes, but the signal is narrower than a generic ``sprint risk'' claim. Late closure is only moderately discriminable and varies across projects. For nonempty sprints, initial scope is strongly associated with unfinished work; a simple scope-only logistic model reaches 0.885 AUC and does not lose to the more complex random forest on the main split. Stricter provenance requirements reduce the sample and weaken some estimates, which makes data history part of the result rather than a footnote. The practical value of these rankings remains a question for prospective validation with real teams.
